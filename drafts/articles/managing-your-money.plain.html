Five simple ways to manage your money better

Sarah Mitchell
3 August 2026

Practical, no-nonsense tips to help you build better financial habits, starting today.

Managing money well doesn't require a finance degree, just a few consistent habits repeated over time. We asked our financial wellbeing team which changes actually stick, and which ones people abandon after a week. The five below came out on top: they take minutes to set up, they don't rely on willpower, and each one compounds over the following months. Start with whichever feels most achievable this week rather than trying all five at once.

1. Track where it goes

Before you can change your spending, you need to see it clearly. Use the NatWest app to review your last three months of transactions and group them by category. Most people are surprised by at least one number, usually something small and frequent rather than one big purchase, because it's the recurring costs that quietly add up over a year without ever feeling significant in the moment.

This isn't about guilt or cutting everything out. It's simply about having accurate information before you decide what, if anything, to change. Plenty of people find their spending is already sensible once they see it laid out clearly, and the exercise just gives them peace of mind rather than a list of cuts to make.

Use category tracking

The app automatically sorts your spending into categories like groceries, transport and entertainment, so you can spot patterns without doing the maths yourself. Look at three consecutive months rather than one, since a single month can be skewed by a one-off expense like a birthday gift or a car repair, and comparing months side by side makes genuine trends much easier to spot.

I didn't realise how much I was spending on takeaways until I saw it broken down by category. That one insight paid for itself in the first month.

2. Automate your savings

Set up a standing order that moves money into a savings account the day you get paid, so saving happens before you have a chance to spend it. Even a small, consistent amount adds up faster than people expect, and because it happens automatically, it stops being a decision you have to make and remember every single month.

Behavioural research consistently shows that automated saving outperforms manual saving, largely because it removes the moment of temptation entirely. If the money has already moved before your first weekend of the month, there's no internal debate about whether to transfer it or spend it instead.

Pick the right day

Time the transfer for the day after payday, not the day of. That gives your salary a moment to clear and avoids any awkward timing issues with your bank, particularly around holidays or weekends when payments can be delayed by a day or two without warning.

3. Build a small emergency fund

Even a few hundred pounds set aside can stop an unexpected bill from becoming a crisis. Start small and build it up gradually rather than waiting until you can save a large lump sum, because that all-or-nothing mindset is exactly what causes most people to never start saving an emergency fund at all.

How much is enough?

A good starting target is one month of essential expenses, covering rent or mortgage, bills, and groceries. Once you hit that, you can decide whether to keep building toward three months of cover or redirect the money toward another goal, such as a deposit or a big purchase you're planning for later in the year.

Having even £500 set aside meant a broken boiler was an inconvenience, not a disaster.

4. Review your subscriptions

Recurring payments add up quietly. A quarterly check-in can uncover services you no longer use, from streaming platforms to app subscriptions you forgot you signed up for during a free trial months ago. Once a quarter, scroll through your last statement and highlight every recurring payment, then ask yourself honestly whether you've actually used each one in the past month. If the answer is no, cancel it there and then rather than telling yourself you'll get around to it later.

5. Set one clear goal

Whether it's a deposit for a first home or a holiday fund, a specific target makes it easier to stay motivated. Vague goals like "save more" are much harder to stick to than a concrete number and date, because there's no way to measure progress or know when you've actually succeeded.

Name your savings pot after the goal and check in on your progress once a month. Seeing the number grow, even slowly, is one of the simplest and most reliable ways to stay motivated over the months it takes to reach a meaningful target.
